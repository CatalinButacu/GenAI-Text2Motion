\chapter{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

The previous three chapters each closed with the threats specific to one contribution. This chapter covers the limits that apply to the thesis as a whole.

\section{The Data-Constrained Regime}
\label{sec:disc_data}

The largest structural limit of this work is neither the GPU nor the choice of sequence mixer. It is that captioned motion capture does not exist at the scale that the model sizes of this field imply. This section counts the data and relates the count to the training behaviour reported in \chapref{chap:generator}.

\paragraph{The token budget.} The tokenizer emits one latent step per $d = 4$ frames, with $Q = 8$ codes per step, and the generator has one cross-entropy target per code. With $F$ the number of training frames after windowing and $m = 2$ the mirror factor, the number of training targets is
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{F}{d} \cdot Q \cdot m .
\end{equation}
The HumanML3D training split has $F = 1{,}631{,}508$ frames over $11{,}692$ base clips, which gives $D = 6.53$ million tokens. The final generators train on HumanML3D alone (\secref{sec:gen_training}).

\paragraph{Against compute-optimal scaling.} Hoffmann et al.~\cite{hoffmann2022chinchilla} fit the loss of language models as $L(N, D) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}$ in parameters $N$ and tokens $D$, and find that for a fixed compute budget the optimum lies near $D^\star \approx 20N$. Read literally, a generator with $15$M parameters would call for about $310$ million tokens, roughly fifty times what HumanML3D provides.

Repeating the data does not close this gap. Muennighoff et al.~\cite{muennighoff2023dataconstrained} model the value of repeated tokens as decaying, with $R$ the number of epochs and $R^\star_D \approx 15$:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{eff}} = D\Bigl(1 + R^\star_D\bigl(1 - e^{-(R-1)/R^\star_D}\bigr)\Bigr) \;\xrightarrow[R \to \infty]{}\; D\,(1 + R^\star_D).
\end{equation}
For $D = 6.53$M this caps the effective budget at about $107$ million tokens however long we train. The parameter count that such a budget supports under the same ratio is about $5.4$M. Our generators, with $15.30$M and $15.55$M parameters, are about three times larger than that.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic restatement.} The training split carries at most $D \log_2 V = 65.3$~Mbit, or $8.2$~MB, of information, and its true entropy is much lower because motion at a 5~Hz latent rate is strongly autocorrelated. At the storage capacity of about two bits per parameter measured for language models~\cite{allenzhu2024capacity}, a model with $15$M parameters can hold about $31$~Mbit, half of the upper bound on the whole training set. Since the true entropy is much lower than that bound, a model of this size can memorise a large part of the corpus.

This agrees with the training of \chapref{chap:generator}: the state-space generator had its best validation FID at epoch 24 and its lowest clean validation cross-entropy at epoch 35, and the stop rule ended its run at epoch 41. The transformer improved until the end of its 60-epoch schedule. A longer schedule cannot add distinct motion; more data can.

\paragraph{Why the constants should not be read literally.} Three reservations apply. The ratio $D^\star \approx 20N$ is compute-optimal, not a requirement: with data fixed, a larger model can still reach a lower loss. Its constants were fitted on English text, and no such fit exists for motion tokens, so the functional form transfers and the number 20 need not. And part of what the model knows is imported: the CLIP text encoder was pretrained on a very large image--text corpus and is not learned from 6.53 million tokens. The field as a whole is over-parameterised at this data scale in the same way; heavy regularisation and early stopping on validation are what make that workable.

\paragraph{Bearing on the claims.} This limit bounds the absolute quality we can report. It does not threaten the controlled comparison: both generators have nearly the same number of parameters and the same data, so over-parameterisation applies to them identically. It does shape the future work of \chapref{chap:conclusion}, which is weighted towards more data and away from larger models.

\section{Statistical Limits: Seeds, Test Reuse and Evaluator Equivalence}
\label{sec:disc_statistics}

\paragraph{Seeds.} Every training result in this thesis is one run with seed 2026. The 20 repetitions of the evaluation protocol average over sampling noise in generation and in the metrics; they say nothing about how much a result would move under a different initialisation or data order. The statement supported by our evidence is therefore: \emph{in one matched run, the two generators had equal text match, the transformer had a lower FID (paired difference $-0.036$, $95\%$ interval $[-0.065, -0.011]$), and the state-space generator had more foot sliding}. A statement about the expected ordering over seeds would need more runs. This is the first experiment we would add.

\paragraph{Test reuse.} Model selection used the validation split only. The final pair was scored on test once, after the selection. Earlier generators of the project, with other recipes, were also scored on test under an older protocol; this thesis does not report them.

\paragraph{Validation and test disagree in level.} On the selected checkpoints, the 600-clip validation FID is higher than the test FID on 4648 segments for both generators ($0.149$ against $0.085$ for the transformer, $0.153$ against $0.110$ for the state-space model). Both use the same generation procedure, so the difference comes from the size and the definition of the two populations: the test population includes the mirrored clips and the segments of the reference code. FID is biased upward at smaller sample sizes, which is why we compare FID values only at equal clip counts and treat the validation figure as a selection signal, not as an estimate of test quality.

\paragraph{Evaluator equivalence.} Comparability with published numbers rests on using the same evaluator in the same way. Our harness reproduces the published R-precision of real motion on the 2189 unmirrored test clips ($0.514$ against $0.511$). An earlier version of the harness did not, and gave R-precision values that were too low; the results of \tabref{tab:twin_results} and \tabref{tab:twin_paired} were produced after that was corrected. Cited rows come from other training runs and are treated as context, not as the other arm of a controlled comparison.

\section{Scope Limits: Whole-Body Motion and Model Scale}
\label{sec:disc_scope}

\paragraph{Whole-body motion.} The quantitative results concern the 22-joint body. The viewer draws an SMPL-X surface, but the hands and face are not generated.

\paragraph{Model scale.} The two final generators have about $15$M parameters each. Earlier in the project we trained a matched pair with 34M parameters and a 100M-parameter transformer without a matching state-space model. These used other recipes and an older protocol, so we report none of them. The analysis of \secref{sec:disc_data} suggests that on this corpus additional parameters would mostly add capacity to memorise, but that is an argument, not a measurement.

\paragraph{Absolute quality.} The best bidirectional model is far ahead of both of our generators in FID ($0.045$ against $0.085$ and $0.110$). Streaming has a price in quality at present, and this thesis does not remove it. What it shows is the price of constant memory between two streaming designs: equal text match, and a small but significant loss in FID and foot sliding (\tabref{tab:twin_paired}).

\paragraph{Physical plausibility.} Generated motion has about $50\%$ (transformer) and $60\%$ (state-space) more foot sliding than real motion (\tabref{tab:twin_paired}). The final generators have no loss term for foot contact, and we do not enforce contact or balance constraints.

\paragraph{Data licensing and use.} AMASS, SMPL-X and HumanML3D are distributed under research licences that restrict redistribution; this work uses them for research only and redistributes none of them. A text-to-motion model can be used to animate a body performing actions a real person did not perform. The models here generate a generic skeleton and carry no identity, which limits that risk, but it is a property of the application and should be considered by anyone who builds on it.
